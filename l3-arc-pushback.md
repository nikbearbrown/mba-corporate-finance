# Pushback on /l3 — Corporate Finance with AI

The doc is well-built. The pushback below is at the load-bearing joints, not the surface. CLAUDE.md's reflexive commitment says the method applies to the method — and a doc marked "Status: Locked" before /l4, /c1, /c2 have been done is exactly the kind of premature settlement that commitment was written to prevent. So treat this as the structural review the lock should pass through before it actually locks.

## 1. The 3-5-7 asymmetry is described as a risk and then accepted without engineering

The doc names the asymmetry, names the adopter problem, and shrugs. But the natural two-semester split — Ch 1-8 in fall, Ch 9-15 in spring — has a real consequence the doc doesn't reckon with.

Semester 1 ends after Ch 8 (Real-World Capital Structure). The student has spent fourteen weeks learning to read firms, evaluate projects, compute WACC, and understand capital structure theory. She has *not yet* recommended a payout policy, walked an IPO, valued an M&A target, hedged a currency exposure, or produced an integrated capstone memo. She has the intellectual scaffolding of a corporate finance course and none of its decision payoff.

That's not a complete module. That's a course that was paused at the worst possible moment — right when the abstractions were about to start cashing out into CFO work.

The fragmentability claim — "End of Act One could be a three-week module" — has the same problem inverted. Three weeks of reading firms with no act of allocation is a financial-statement-analysis module wearing a corporate-finance label.

**The honest version:** the book is not actually adoptable in fragments without the capstone payoff. Either accept that and stop pitching adoption flexibility, or restructure so each act ends with a small integrated decision (not full capstone, but a CFO recommendation in miniature) so a fragment is recoverable.

## 2. The Ch 7-8 "two-chapter pivot" claim is over-priced

MM → frictions is the standard pedagogical sequence in every corporate finance textbook from Brealey-Myers to Berk-DeMarzo to Ross-Westerfield. Calling it the "conceptual pivot of the book" — equivalent in load to Book 1's Diversification Miracle — over-states the difficulty.

Diversification is genuinely counterintuitive. A student who hasn't seen it does not predict the result. MM is an idealization students intuit immediately ("yeah obviously if there are no taxes and no bankruptcy costs, it doesn't matter"). The interesting work isn't the pivot from Ch 7 to Ch 8 — it's getting students to take MM seriously as a *baseline they reason against* rather than a result they dismiss as unrealistic and forget.

That's a different teaching problem than the doc names. The risk isn't that students fail to make the leap from Ch 7 to Ch 8. The risk is that they skim Ch 7, treat MM as a textbook ritual, and arrive at Ch 8 with no baseline against which to measure the frictions. Coordinating the two chapters as a unit doesn't fix this; it can make it worse, because the second chapter is where students think the "real" content lives.

**The fix to consider:** Don't write Ch 7-8 as a paired pivot. Write Ch 7 with worked examples that *force* students to use MM as a measuring stick — make them quantify the value of the tax shield in Ch 7 itself, before frictions formally arrive. Then Ch 8's deviations land on a foundation the student has already used.

## 3. "Inside the firm" vs "outside the firm" does less work than the doc claims

Standard MBA corporate finance already operates from inside-firm perspective. FCFF and FCFE are inside-firm constructs. Capital budgeting projects are inside-firm decisions. Working capital is inside-firm by definition. The Berk-DeMarzo and Brealey-Myers textbooks do not stand outside the firm; they sit inside it.

The doc treats the inside/outside distinction as Book 2's distinguishing pedagogical claim. It isn't. The actual distinguishing feature is the AI-native CFO recommendation discipline — the verification chain, the three-beat method, the Excel-rejection thesis. That's what makes this book different from Brealey-Myers.

**The risk of leaning on the inside/outside frame:** a faculty reviewer who teaches corporate finance will read "we put Maya inside the firm" and think *we already do that*. The differentiation argument fails on first contact. Lead with what's actually different.

## 4. Maya continuity for fresh readers is a requirement without a mechanism

"The chapter must work for both [Book 1 returnees and fresh starters]" is named and abandoned. There's no proposal for how. The two failure modes are concrete:

- Re-introduce Maya, the three-beat method, and the verification toolkit in full → the Book 1 returnee skips the first 30% of Ch 1, and the chapter loses its load-bearing reader.
- Don't re-introduce → the fresh reader has no character, no method, and no toolkit, and Ch 1 fails at its onboarding job.

The middle path — a sidebar, an appendix, an "if you're new" callout — is a structural choice that needs to be designed, not waved at. This is the kind of thing /c1 should solve, and locking /l3 before that work is done assumes the solution exists. It doesn't yet.

## 5. Ch 14 is set up to fail and the doc admits it without solving it

The doc writes: "Many readers will be tempted to skip the chapter or read it superficially." Then it proposes one mitigation: position Ch 14 right before Ch 15. That doesn't fix the problem; it intensifies it. A reader two weeks from finals or final paper, looking at a 14th chapter on biases right before the capstone, will skim. The position makes skipping more likely, not less.

If behavioral discipline is required for the capstone — and the doc says it is — then it shouldn't be a standalone chapter at the end of the book. It should be a recurring practice woven through Acts 2 and 3, with Ch 14 as the *consolidation* rather than the introduction. By the time students reach Ch 14, every M&A example in Ch 11 should have already prompted "what bias is this CFO at risk of?" Every dividend decision in Ch 9 should have already named the bias risk.

Standalone Ch 14 is the structural choice most likely to produce the failure the doc names. The fix is to distribute the load.

## 6. Ch 15 capstone overload is the unlearned lesson from Book 1

The doc says: "Same as Book 1's capstone risk — synthesis is hard." Then doesn't propose a structural change in response. If Book 1's capstone was hard with fewer chapters to integrate, Book 2's capstone with seven Act 3 chapters plus everything from Acts 1-2 is harder by a measurable margin.

Two structural options the doc should at least consider:

- **Split Ch 15 into Ch 15A (capstone framework) and Ch 15B (applied case).** Treat synthesis as a two-chapter unit, paralleling the Ch 7-8 pivot logic the doc already endorses.
- **Make Ch 15 narrower.** Don't try to integrate the full curriculum. Integrate the four load-bearing decisions (capital budgeting, capital structure recommendation, payout, one strategic transaction) and let the rest be in-context exercises in their own chapters.

Letting Ch 15 attempt full integration is the path most likely to produce the same capstone weakness Book 1 had.

## 7. The CFO frame is aspirational beyond most readers' near-term work

Most MBA finance students will not be CFOs in the next decade. They'll be analysts at investment banks, founders, PMs at startups, consultants, treasurers, FP&A leads. The CFO frame is good for narrative coherence — it gives Maya a clear seat to occupy — but the *capability* the book is teaching is more general.

What if the capability is "produce an integrated capital allocation memo that a CFO would defend"? That's neutral on whether the producer is the CFO, the CFO's analyst, the consultant pitching the CFO, or the founder asking the question of herself. It scopes the book to the capability rather than the title.

This may not need a structural change. It might just be a framing adjustment in Ch 1 and the capstone. But the current "defensible CFO recommendation" language is doing identity work that the actual exercises probably don't need.

## 8. "Status: Locked" violates the workshop's own reflexive commitment

CLAUDE.md, §12: *"Do not treat this file as settled authority. Treat it as the current instrument."*

The /l3 lock is premature for at least three reasons:

- /l4 (prerequisites), /c1 (chapter docs), and /c2 (chapter anatomy) haven't been done. Working through Ch 7 might reveal it should be three chapters or one. Working through Ch 14's integration into the rest of the book might reveal it shouldn't be standalone. Locking the arc before structural validation is locking before the load tests.
- The 3-5-7 asymmetry has the adoption fragility analyzed above. That's a structural concern that should be resolved before the arc locks, not after.
- The Ch 14 / Ch 15 sequencing has the failure mode admitted but not solved. Locking with an admitted failure mode is locking a known bug.

The lock isn't wrong because the doc is wrong. It's wrong because the workshop's own method says nothing locks until it's been used to do real work. The /l3 has been planned, not yet tested.

## What to do with this

The doc is good. The pushback above doesn't require throwing it out — it requires unlocking long enough to address three things before /l4:

1. Either restructure to make act-fragments genuinely adoptable, or stop claiming fragment adoption.
2. Either distribute behavioral discipline through Acts 2-3 or accept Ch 14 will be skimmed.
3. Either split Ch 15 or narrow its integration scope.

The Ch 7-8 pivot question and the inside/outside framing are smaller calls. The Maya continuity question is real but solvable in /c1. The CFO frame is a language adjustment, not a structural one.

After those three structural calls land, lock.
