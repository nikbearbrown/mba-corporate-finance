# Corporate Finance with AI — /l3 Three-Act Learning Arc

**Status:** Locked
**Sibling project to:** Computational Finance with AI
**Architecture:** 15 chapters, three acts of 3-5-7 chapters

## The Arc Statement

This book takes the reader from "I know what stocks and bonds are but I have no idea how a CFO actually allocates capital" to "I can produce a defensible CFO recommendation that integrates investment, financing, and payout decisions" by first teaching her to read the firm from inside, then teaching her to allocate capital across investments and financing structures, then teaching her to distribute capital and execute the strategic decisions that define a CFO's role.

## Act One — Read the Firm (Chapters 1-3)

### What Act One does

Establishes the inside-the-firm perspective that distinguishes Book 2 from Book 1. Where Book 1 Maya analyzed publicly traded assets from outside, Book 2 Maya is sitting in the CFO's office looking at her own firm's books. By the end of Act One, she can read a company the way a CFO reads it — with operational awareness of working capital and an eye for where reported numbers diverge from operational reality.

### The inciting question

"What does it actually look like inside a real company's finances, and what would I see if I were the CFO?"

This is the question Maya doesn't yet know how to ask before opening Book 2. Her MBA finance core has taught her ratio analysis from outside; this book teaches her the inside view that drives every CFO decision in subsequent acts.

### The opening pebble

Chapter 1 opens with Maya's CFO asking her to evaluate $50M debt issuance versus equity issuance. The pebble is a real CFO decision — one that requires understanding the firm from inside. The chapter walks her through the three-beat method on this decision and reveals how much she doesn't yet know about reading firms from a CFO's seat.

### What Maya knows at the end of Act One

- The three-beat method as a daily working discipline (inherited from Book 1, but rehearsed here for CFO contexts)
- How to read financial statements as a CFO would, not as an outside analyst would
- How to track and interpret working capital metrics for any firm
- How to identify where reported numbers diverge from operational reality
- The verification techniques the book uses, applied to corporate-finance contexts

She does NOT yet know:

- How to evaluate capital projects beyond the simple TVM exposure from her MBA core
- How to think about the firm's overall cost of capital
- How to recommend capital structure changes
- How to handle the major strategic transactions (M&A, IPO) that define CFO careers

### Act One's hardest moment

Chapter 1 is the hardest moment of Act One — same pattern as Book 1. The setup load (Claude account if she hasn't already from Book 1, the three-beat method rehearsed for CFO contexts, the verification toolkit) plus the Excel-rejection thesis adapted for corporate finance plus the worked example all land in one chapter.

A reader who already completed Book 1 has a lighter Chapter 1 experience because much of the setup is already done. A reader starting fresh with Book 2 has the full Chapter 1 load. The chapter must work for both.

### Act One → Act Two Transition

**What must be true for Maya to cross from Act One to Act Two**

1. She can read a firm's statements with the inside view. Not as a fancy ratio exercise — as the orientation a CFO has when she walks into her office.
2. The three-beat method is a working habit. Same standard as Book 1.
3. She has internalized that working capital is the daily reality. Not just understood; felt. Working capital decisions affect cash every day.
4. She is asking new questions. "Now that I can read this firm, what should we do with our money? What projects should we fund?" If she's not asking by the end of Chapter 3, the transition will fail.

**How the book engineers the transition**

Chapter 3 (Working Capital Management) closes by establishing that working capital management frees up cash — and once cash is freed up, the next question is what to do with it. The bridge to Chapter 4 (Capital Budgeting) is natural: working capital management generates internal capital; capital budgeting decides how to deploy it.

**Risk if the transition fails**

If Maya doesn't develop the inside-the-firm view in Act One, the capital allocation chapters in Act Two feel like Book 1 chapters about external companies rather than CFO chapters about her own firm. The book loses its CFO-pedagogy distinctiveness.

## Act Two — Allocate Capital (Chapters 4-8)

### What Act Two does

Builds Maya's capability to allocate capital across investments and financing structures. This act covers both sides of the balance sheet — what to fund (Ch 4-6) and how to fund it (Ch 7-8). The conceptual pivot of the book happens within this act.

### The inciting question

"How does a CFO decide what to fund and how to finance it, and what makes some firms better at this than others?"

This is the question Act One creates. Maya now knows how to read the firm; Act Two teaches her to make the firm's defining capital decisions.

### Act Two's structure

- Chapter 4 (Capital Budgeting) — the investment decision in firm-level form
- Chapter 5 (Cost of Capital and WACC) — the discount rate that links investment and financing
- Chapter 6 (Risk-Adjusted Rates and Real Options) — refines Ch 5 by acknowledging project-specific risk and managerial flexibility
- Chapter 7 (Capital Structure Theory) — first half of conceptual pivot; MM theorems as baseline
- Chapter 8 (Real-World Capital Structure) — second half of conceptual pivot; frictions that make MM-irrelevance fail in practice

### What Maya knows at the end of Act Two

- How to evaluate a firm-level capital project with all its corporate complications
- How to calculate and apply cost of capital with appropriate risk adjustments
- How to value real options and recognize when traditional NPV understates project value
- Modigliani-Miller theorems as the baseline for capital structure thinking
- How real-world frictions (taxes, distress costs, asymmetric information) shape actual capital structure
- How to recommend a capital structure change with quantified value impact

She does NOT yet know:

- How firms return capital to shareholders (dividends, buybacks)
- How firms raise external capital (IPOs, secondary offerings, debt issuance)
- How firms execute major strategic transactions (M&A)
- How firms manage operational financial risks
- How cross-border operations complicate everything
- How behavioral patterns distort even rigorous analysis

### Act Two's hardest moment

The transition from Chapter 6 to Chapter 7 — moving from project-level decisions to firm-level capital structure thinking. This is the conceptual leap of Book 2, parallel to Book 1's Diversification Miracle moment but with a different character.

The leap is engineered through the two-chapter pivot unit (Ch 7-8). Ch 7 establishes MM-irrelevance as the theoretical baseline. Ch 8 shows the deviations in practice. Together they teach Maya to hold theory and practice simultaneously — the core CFO mental discipline.

### Act Two → Act Three Transition

**What must be true for Maya to cross from Act Two to Act Three**

1. She thinks in firm-level capital flows. Not as a math exercise — as how a CFO sees the world.
2. She holds MM as a baseline lightly. Not dismissed; not embraced. Held as the reference point against which real-world deviations are measured.
3. She trusts her verification discipline across capital structure recommendations. Capital structure decisions are high-stakes; she has practice making them with appropriate epistemic humility.
4. She is asking new questions. "Now that we can fund things and structure financing, what about returning capital to shareholders? What about big strategic moves like M&A? What about the risks that could destroy what we've built?" If she's not asking these by the end of Ch 8, Act Three's relevance is unclear.

**How the book engineers the transition**

Chapter 8 (Real-World Capital Structure) closes by foreshadowing what's missing: Maya now knows how to fund the firm, but she doesn't yet know how to return capital (Ch 9), how to raise capital from outside (Ch 10), or how to execute major transactions (Ch 11). The bridge to Ch 9 is explicit: "We've covered how the firm gets capital from internal operations and how it structures external funding. Next: what does the firm do when it has more cash than it can productively reinvest?"

**Risk if the transition fails**

If Maya stays in pure investment-and-financing mode without crossing into payout, transactional, and integrated decisions, the rest of the book feels like a topical tour rather than a CFO education. The capstone in Ch 15 then fails because integration requires comfort across all the decision categories Act Three covers.

## Act Three — Distribute and Execute (Chapters 9-15)

### What Act Three does

Synthesizes everything Maya has learned into the full CFO decision portfolio. This is the longest act because the corporate finance curriculum has more "applied" topics than the investments curriculum: payout, security issuance, M&A, risk management, international, behavioral, and the integrated capstone all live here.

### The inciting question

"What are all the strategic decisions a CFO actually makes, and how do they integrate into a coherent leadership of the firm's financial strategy?"

### Act Three's structure

This is the long act, organized into three sub-arcs:

**Sub-arc 1 — Capital Distribution and External Capital (Ch 9-10)**

- Ch 9 (Dividend Policy) — returning capital to shareholders
- Ch 10 (Security Issuance and IPOs) — raising capital from outside

These two chapters form a natural pair: capital flows out (Ch 9) and capital flows in (Ch 10). Together they complete the picture of capital movement between firm and investors.

**Sub-arc 2 — Strategic Transactions (Ch 11)**

- Ch 11 (M&A) — the largest single corporate finance decisions

M&A integrates everything from Acts One and Two — it requires reading the target firm, valuing it, structuring the financing, and managing the transition. It's the natural single-chapter culmination of analytical capability before risk and integration take over.

**Sub-arc 3 — Risk, Context, and Integration (Ch 12-15)**

- Ch 12 (Risk Management) — operational financial risk discipline
- Ch 13 (International Corporate Finance) — cross-border layer
- Ch 14 (Behavioral Corporate Finance) — the human element
- Ch 15 (Capstone) — synthesis

This sub-arc moves from operational discipline (Ch 12) to environmental complexity (Ch 13) to human reality (Ch 14) to integration (Ch 15).

### What Maya knows at the end of Act Three

- How firms decide between dividends, buybacks, and retention
- How firms access public capital markets through IPOs and secondary offerings
- How M&A transactions are valued, structured, and integrated
- How firms manage operational financial risks
- How cross-border operations introduce currency, country, and regulatory complications
- How managerial biases distort even rigorous corporate finance decisions
- How to integrate everything into a defensible CFO recommendation

She has the capability the book promised in Chapter 1.

### Act Three's hardest moments

Two hard moments in this act, not one:

**Hard moment 1: Chapter 14 (Behavioral Corporate Finance).** This chapter breaks the analytical pattern of the book. Where every prior chapter taught Maya to do analysis better, this chapter teaches her to recognize when analysis isn't enough. The epistemic shift is real and uncomfortable. Many readers will be tempted to skip the chapter or read it superficially.

**Hard moment 2: Chapter 15 (Capstone).** Same as Book 1's capstone risk — synthesis is hard. The capstone in Book 2 has even more to integrate than Book 1's because Act Three is longer.

The mitigation for both: Ch 14 is positioned immediately before Ch 15 specifically so that the capstone is built with behavioral honesty, not after it. The capstone's verification chain section explicitly includes behavioral risk identification as a mandatory verification step.

## Resourcing Implications (Final)

The four highest-priority writing efforts in Book 2:

1. Chapter 1 (Introduction) — onboarding load
2. Chapter 7 (Capital Structure Theory) — first half of conceptual pivot
3. Chapter 8 (Real-World Capital Structure) — second half of conceptual pivot
4. Chapter 15 (Capstone) — synthesis

Together these four chapters consume 30-40% of the book's writing effort even though they're 27% of the chapters.

Special note on Ch 7-8: These two chapters must be written together, not sequentially. The pedagogy requires them to flow as a single conceptual unit. A draft of Ch 7 alone is not a complete draft because the lessons of Ch 7 are unstable until Ch 8 establishes the practical context.

## What Each Act's "Closing Capability" Means for Adoption

For faculty considering adoption:

- **End of Act One (Ch 3):** A faculty member could stop here and assign Ch 1-3 as a three-week module on "reading the firm as a CFO." This is a teachable unit on its own and might serve as introductory content for an MBA Corporate Finance core.
- **End of Act Two (Ch 8):** Adding Ch 4-8 produces a complete capital-allocation module. This is a natural "investment and financing" course scope, common in MBA programs that split corporate finance across two terms.
- **End of Act Three (Ch 15):** The full Part 1 (15 chapters) is the complete MBA Corporate Finance course textbook.

The act structure means Book 2 is also adoptable in fragments. A program teaching corporate finance across two semesters might assign Ch 1-8 in the first semester and Ch 9-15 in the second.

## Confirmation of the Arc

The arc statement at the top is testable. Each act has:

- A clear inciting question
- A specific capability built
- Named transitions to the next act
- Risks of failure articulated
- Resourcing implications identified

The arc is internally coherent: Maya progresses from reading the firm (Act One) to allocating capital (Act Two) to distributing capital and executing strategic decisions (Act Three). Each act builds on the previous one. The transitions are engineered moments, especially the two-chapter pivot in Act Two.

The arc is externally defensible: a faculty reviewer can read this and see how the course unfolds across a semester. The act boundaries align with natural pacing milestones, though the asymmetric act lengths (3-5-7 chapters) require explicit pacing guidance for adopters.

The arc serves the thesis: at no point does the book retreat into Excel-based pedagogy. Every act demonstrates AI-native CFO work with verification discipline.
